Two text classifiers built from scratch — a
[naive Bayes][naive-bayes]
bag-of-words model and a set of
[n-gram][n-gram] language models — that
learn word statistics from a corpus and label unseen documents by them.
Both estimate probabilities by counting, and both smooth those counts so
an unseen word does not zero out a whole document.

**Naive Bayes picks the most probable class.** For a document $d$ and
classes $c$, the classifier returns the maximum a posteriori class.
Dropping the shared denominator and assuming words are conditionally
independent given the class,

$$
\hat{c} = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{c}\; P(c) \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i \mid c),
$$

where $P(c)$ is the class prior and $P(w_i \mid c)$ is the likelihood of
word $w_i$ under class $c$. The product hides a modeling choice: the
**bag-of-words** representation treats a document as its multiset of word
counts with position discarded, so $P(d \mid c) = \prod_i P(w_i \mid c)$
scores each token as an independent draw from the class's word
distribution. That independence is wrong — words in text are correlated —
but it keeps estimation to simple counts and still classifies well.
Products of many small probabilities underflow, so the implementation
works in log space, where the product becomes a sum:
$\log P(c) + \sum_i \log P(w_i \mid c)$, and the $\operatorname*{arg\,max}$
is unchanged.

**Smoothing handles unseen words.** A word absent from a class in
training gives $P(w \mid c) = 0$ and annihilates the product. Add-$k$
(Laplace) smoothing shifts every count up by $k$:

$$
P(w \mid c) = \frac{\operatorname{count}(w, c) + k}{\sum_{w'} \operatorname{count}(w', c) + k\lvert V \rvert},
$$

with $V$ the vocabulary. The numerator keeps an unseen word from zeroing
the whole product, and the matching term in the denominator keeps the row
summing to one, so smoothing rescues the estimate without breaking it into
an improper distribution. Small $k$ trusts the counts; large $k$ pulls
every word toward uniform.

**Binary naive Bayes.** For tasks like sentiment, whether a word appears
matters more than how many times. Boolean naive Bayes clips each
document's counts to presence or absence, $\min(\operatorname{count}(w, d),
1)$, before estimating and scoring, so a review repeating _great_ ten
times cannot swamp the decision on its own. On short texts this variant
often edges out the count-based model.

**The pipeline, end to end.** The classifier is four counting steps:

- **Tokenize.** Split each document into words and reduce it to its bag of
  counts.
- **Estimate the prior.** Set $P(c)$ from the fraction of training
  documents in each class.
- **Estimate the likelihoods.** Compute each $P(w \mid c)$ as an add-$k$
  smoothed count ratio over the class's text.
- **Score.** Sum $\log P(c) + \sum_i \log P(w_i \mid c)$ for every class
  and return the $\operatorname*{arg\,max}$.

**N-grams model the next word.** The n-gram side estimates the
probability of a sequence with the chain rule under a Markov assumption
— each word depends only on the previous $n-1$:

$$
P(w_1 \ldots w_m) \approx \prod_{i=1}^{m} P(w_i \mid w_{i-n+1} \ldots w_{i-1}).
$$

Each conditional is a smoothed count ratio, exactly as above but keyed on
the preceding context. Larger $n$ captures more context but fragments the
counts, so unigram, bigram, and trigram models trade coverage against
sharpness. Used as a classifier, the model trained on each class scores a
test document, and the class whose model assigns higher probability wins.
